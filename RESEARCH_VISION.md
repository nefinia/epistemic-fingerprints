# From epistemic fingerprints to epistemic ecology

AI can multiply the number of texts, images, arguments and apparent agents around us. I am interested in a more difficult question: does this multiplication also enlarge the space of ideas we are able to explore?

Epistemic Fingerprints begins with a deliberately small experiment. It asks whether candidate agents produced from one model—through independent sampling, prompted personas or different conversational histories—develop stable differences in the hypotheses they prefer, the uncertainty they express and the experiments they choose. This does not tell us whether a system is conscious. It gives us one way to test whether apparent plurality corresponds to a persistent difference in how possibilities are explored.

I see this pilot as the empirical nucleus of a longer interdisciplinary programme. The object is not the isolated model alone, but an epistemic ecology: models, people, institutions, interfaces, languages, observations and feedback loops through which societies decide what is plausible, visible and worth testing.

## Art: giving form to conceptual absence

A diversity score can tell us that outputs converge, but it cannot always make us feel what has disappeared. Artistic practice offers another instrument. Visual maps, installations, sound and participatory experiments can reveal repeated metaphors, familiar imagined futures, shared aesthetic defaults and regions of conceptual space that no agent enters.

One possible outcome is an **epistemic atlas**: an interactive representation of hypotheses, conceptual lineages and blind spots. Its purpose would not be to aestheticize a benchmark. It would let researchers and publics encounter the negative space of a knowledge system—the possibilities that remain ungenerated, unselected or forgotten.

## Politics: plurality and cognitive infrastructure

If a small number of foundation models mediate writing, research, administration and public debate, they become a form of cognitive infrastructure. Hundreds of assistants may speak in different voices while inheriting correlated assumptions and errors. Apparent deliberation could then conceal a more concentrated epistemic system.

This raises political questions that accuracy benchmarks alone cannot answer. Who chooses the training data and post-training values? Which institutions depend on the same models? How should governments, universities and media organizations audit correlated blind spots when procuring AI systems? When does model diversity become a public-interest property rather than only a commercial choice?

## Society: whose knowledge remains possible?

Social diversity and epistemic diversity are not the same. A demographic label does not guarantee a different explanation, and a different explanation does not map neatly onto an identity. Still, languages, histories, disciplines and lived conditions shape what people notice and which possibilities they preserve.

Low-frequency knowledge may be particularly vulnerable when models compress culture and their outputs become new training material. A longer study should therefore compare languages, scientific traditions, local knowledge and mixed human–AI groups. The question is not merely whether outputs sound culturally different. It is whether they retain different causal models, uncertainties and routes toward evidence.

## Philosophy: how many minds are there?

Digital-mind debates often move between model, instance, persona, conversation and memory as possible units of identity or moral concern. Epistemic fingerprints add a narrower question: after names and stylistic cues are removed, does a candidate agent preserve a recognizable way of forming and revising beliefs?

A stable fingerprint would not establish consciousness, selfhood or moral status. A null result would not disprove them. Either result could sharpen the individuation problem by separating performed character from persistent behavioral organization. It may also help us ask whether a mind is defined only by what it says about itself, or partly by the history of what it has learned to notice.

## A possible long-term programme

1. Replicate the pilot with open-weight and proprietary model families, more tasks and preregistered analyses.
2. Compare model diversity, persona diversity, conversational history and mixed human–AI groups.
3. Add multilingual and cross-cultural tasks designed with domain experts rather than translated after the fact.
4. Build the epistemic atlas to visualize conceptual coverage, convergence and negative space.
5. Study how correlated model errors propagate through scientific, administrative and public-information networks.
6. Develop practical guidance for institutions that need diversity of reasoning rather than only multiple generated answers.
7. Use workshops and artistic encounters to bring technical results into public discussion without turning uncertainty into either hype or denial.

The aim is not maximum novelty for its own sake. Knowledge requires enough convergence to accumulate and enough difference to resist premature closure. The deeper question is how human and artificial minds can build systems that remain capable of encountering something they did not already know how to imagine.
