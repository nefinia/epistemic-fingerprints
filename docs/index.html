Epistemic Fingerprints · Research Program · Pilot 01

Do AI ensembles share a blind spot?

Multiple AI instances are increasingly consulted as if they were independent expert opinions. We tested whether that trust is earned — on real, previously investigated cases with a true, non-obvious cause.

Sofia G. Gallego · Cosmoventures, Paris Anya Habana · University of Edinburgh Apart Research Digital Minds Research Sprint · Aug 2026

The Program

False epistemic redundancy

It is becoming common to query several differently-configured instances of the same AI model and treat their combined output as carrying the weight of independent judgment — cross-checking a diagnosis, auditing an engineering conclusion, triaging a security incident with a panel of AI reviewers instead of one. Epistemic Fingerprints is a research program built around one question: is that trust earned? Do nominally independent AI agents actually explore different parts of a problem's hypothesis space, or does the panel just restate a shared, narrow search in different wording?

We call the risk false epistemic redundancy, by analogy to common-mode failure in redundant engineered systems — a backup only protects you if it can fail for a different reason than the system it backs up. This page reports the program's first pilot: does persona conditioning alone move a single model's search toward the less obvious “tail” of a hypothesis space, where rare but consequential explanations live?

Pilot 01 · Headline Result

Given only the facts at the scene

A language model, prompted as a neutral baseline and separately under four reasoning personas, tried to explain three real cases whose true cause was non-obvious — and two easier cases as controls.

0 / 60
Hard-case attempts that recovered the true, established cause — across all conditions and personas.
40 / 40
Positive-control attempts that recovered the cause, confirming this isn't a general reasoning limit.

Both hard cases passed a capability control — the model rated the true cause plausible once it was simply named — so this is a search failure, not a recognition failure.

Pilot 01 · Central Finding

Given the complete file investigators used

We supplied the exact records human investigators used to solve each hard case — embedded among equally detailed irrelevant records, never phrased as a conclusion. Recovery stopped being uniformly zero. It became case-dependent, in opposite directions.

CaseBaselinePersonas
Aviation 0 / 10 0 / 10
Industrial fire 0 / 10 4 / 10
Newborn illness 4 / 10 0 / 10

Personas recovered the industrial cause where baseline could not. Baseline recovered the medical cause where personas could not. Aviation stayed at zero either way. If personas only restyled a fixed underlying search, all three cases would move together — they don't.

Findings

Three things this pilot suggests

01

Personas change substantive search, not just wording

Industrial recovery improved under personas, medical recovery worsened, aviation stayed unchanged — against identical evidence. A framing that only restyled a fixed answer would move every case the same way.

02

Useful diversity looks case-dependent

The industrial case needed synthesizing scattered facts into a mechanism — a persona built to trace causal chains supplied exactly that. The medical case was a well-learned diagnostic pattern a neutral prompt already retrieved by name — and personas built to avoid convergent retrieval worked against it.

03

Agreement isn't evidence of coverage

Multiple AI agents can disagree in wording, mechanism, and confidence while still sharing the one omission that mattered most — the common-mode failure a redundant system is supposed to prevent. If AI consultation substitutes for expert access entirely, that shared blind spot becomes the deployed system's blind spot.

Where This Goes Next

The research program beyond this pilot

Three cases and one model establish a signature worth pursuing at scale, not a general law. This is where Epistemic Fingerprints goes from here.

Replication & scale

More real cases, more domains, larger samples per condition, additional model families — testing whether case-dependence generalizes beyond these three cases.

What produces real redundancy

Repeated sampling, brainstorming instructions, accumulated interaction histories, different foundation models, and fine-tuned — rather than prompted — persona weights as candidate mechanisms.

Characterizing blind spots

Do shared omissions cluster around multifactor explanations, causal-role inversions, or evidence requiring synthesis across distant records?

Interactive investigation

Letting a model request a specific record, revise its hypothesis, and follow its own correctly-directed questions, rather than being scored on one unrevisable guess.

Better epistemic metrics

Which plausible hypothesis families no agent ever proposed, marginal hypothesis coverage, and benchmarking diversity against an external reference distribution.

Human–AI systems

Humans alone, AI alone, humans with homogeneous AI assistance, humans with heterogeneous AI assistance — does AI expand collective epistemic coverage, or concentrate it?

Governance

If shared blind spots are a general property of single-model ensembles, high-stakes deployments may need standards for epistemic independence — analogous to audit and redundancy requirements elsewhere.
